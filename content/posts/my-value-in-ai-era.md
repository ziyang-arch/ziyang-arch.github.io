When I am working on a complex system, the boundary of what AI can understand and accomplish is difficult to define. I need to flexibly cover the areas that AI misses, acting as its complement.

When I write articles, I don't want to use AI to generate them directly. It feels as if using AI diminishes my sense of existence. Am I simply missing that *feeling* of presence, or does the article itself actually lack something inherently mine?

Imagine if everyone started using AI to assist their writing, would most people eventually become overwhelmingly similar? Then again, this seems to have always been the norm in human society; the expressions of the vast majority have always existed within a common, shared set.

## The Shift Towards High-Level Abstraction

As everyone begins to work with AI, the level of abstraction they must focus on rises alongside the AI's capabilities. They become increasingly like senior engineers or managers.

This raises a question: how much does a manager need to know about the details? In the pre-GPT era, the cultivation of a manager usually started from the junior level. Therefore, a good manager's understanding of the overall technical direction is undoubtedly deeper than that of a junior doing hands-on work.

If, in the AI era, a person's career starts directly at the "manager" level, the primary problem we face is that individuals growing up this way might lack certain traits compared to those who started as juniors. These traits might include a deep understanding of details, or the experience of troubleshooting and avoiding errors.

The subsequent question is: are these abilities still necessary? Are they truly the part of humanity that AI cannot replace? This is actually uncertain. As Geoffrey Hinton exemplified in an interview, major human discoveries often happen when two distant fields intersect within a single person's mind. And this specific capability is precisely one of the areas where AI outperforms humans. (This refers to "Analogical Reasoning," or more broadly, "Cross-domain Association," from his talk *Two Paths to Intelligence*.)

## The Challenge for Education and Assessment

Following this, even if we confirm that this capability is irreplaceable, indispensable, and crucial for steering AI, how do we screen for it during an interview? Drawing parallels to the selection and promotion processes of managers, can this capability truly be assessed within the span of a short interview?

Consequently, we face another problem: when undergraduate or entry-level job seekers are expected to possess senior-level understanding and control, in what direction should university education pivot?

While reflecting on my PhD journey, I suddenly realized the answer: even undergraduate education should shift toward an Ancient Greek-style (or Spring and Autumn period-style) apprenticeship model, gradually abandoning the standardized Prussian education system. This shift is both a change brought about by AI and one empowered by it.

## Knowing the Boundaries

A classmate once said, *"If you try to accomplish everything using AI, it won't be long before they realize they can just use AI to replace you entirely."*

Later, while interacting and working with AI, I thought about this question. Then I suddenly understood: it is precisely *because* I use AI that I understand the boundaries of its capabilities, **what it cannot do**. This is exactly where my value lies. 

I cannot be certain if there will come a day when AI's boundaries stretch so far and develop so quickly that they outpace human intelligence entirely. But for today, I still believe that the things I do are not completely devoid of value.
